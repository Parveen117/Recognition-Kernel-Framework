# PG873 · Rāmacandra’s Subodhinī and the choice of Daśaratha

### The second Subodhinī can be identified independently

Mitra’s *Notices of Sanskrit MSS*, VIII (1886), no. **2777**, printed pp. 220–221, records Rāmacandravācaspati’s commentary on Bhaṭṭi from 202 Bengali-script paper leaves, seven lines to a page. At the time of the catalogue the deposit was with Dvārakānātha Bhaṭṭācārya in Haṃsapukuragrāma, post Kālnā, Vardhamāna. The copy date is unspecified. The transcribed colophon names Rāmacandravācaspati and Subodhinī, identifies canto twenty-two and says the work ends. Entry 2778 on the same page is a commentary on the Rāmagītā. PG871’s 115 palm leaves at Gāṅguṭe are the distinct Kumudānanda record, which Mitra himself cross-references.

Two pieces of Rāmacandra’s own text survive in the catalogue excerpt. Its beginning identifies the king known as Daśaratha and explicitly raises the rule for **liṭ in an unobserved past** after quoting the opening *abhūt*. The extract breaks off with *ityādi* after the objection. Its end recalls King Śrīdharasena, the poet’s receipt of favour and the city of Valabhī. It aligns with the author’s geographical and royal reference at XXII.35. The granting of favour belongs to the commentary’s interpretation. The poem’s cited stanza says the work was composed in Valabhī under that king. The pairing identifies the beginning and end of this particular work. It also gives a specific exemplum of how its author discusses a finite form. Neither excerpt covers VII.103. S1 still needs the words at that locus. The historical notice does not establish which of the two Subodhinīs Mallinātha meant, the modern custody of 2777, or any particular correction promised at the unrelated 3.2.111 data field.

### Two aorists in the first stanza

| Question at I.1 | Evidence and derivation |
| --- | --- |
| Why introduce Daśaratha with *abhūt*? | In the 1887 Jayamaṅgalā (p. 1), the poet gives the king’s existence in the **general past**, for which 3.2.110 supplies luṅ. The commentator separately states the condition on which one would expect liṭ, namely a long-past king beyond the poet’s observation (3.2.115). The 1886 catalogue excerpt of Rāmacandra gives the latter objection but omits its answer. Jayamaṅgalā’s answer has its own owner. Mallinātha (p. 2) glosses the finite form as the king’s coming into existence and explains sic-luk and the absence of guṇa. On 01.0001 bhū, 2.4.77 gives sic-luk before these active endings and 7.3.89 guards the root vowel. Before the nasal of *nṛpaḥ*, the nasalized final consonant in *abhūn nṛpo* is a junction reading under 8.4.45. The already registered unprefixed bhū cell receives no new credit. |
| Who chose whom? | The subject of *upāgamat* is the eternal one, identified as Viṣṇu by both commentators. The relative *yam* refers back to the king, and *pitaram* gives the capacity in which he is chosen. Jayamaṅgalā interprets the choice as accepting him as father and reads *svayam* as agency without prompting. Mallinātha glosses the object as the progenitor. They distinguish Viṣṇu, the agent here, from the king who was subject of *abhūt*. The mutually dependent terms describe one chosen man. |
| For what end? | Jayamaṅgalā explains the apparent benefit to the worlds through Viṣṇu’s avatāras and here the destruction of Rāvaṇa and others. Mallinātha also relates the pretext to the service to the worlds in destroying Rāvaṇa and adds a Bālakāṇḍa comparison for the choice of a father. Keep the compound and this narrative motivation with the father-selection clause. |

For the second predicate the root is bhvādi **01.1137 gamḷ̐ gatau**, parasmaipada, aniṭ. The preverb is **upa**. Both commentaries assign aṅ, Jayamaṅgalā by the root’s lṛ-marker and Mallinātha by citing the puṣādi rule. Thus 3.1.55 supplies aṅ for cli here. The finite stem has gam-a-, with initial past aṭ (6.4.71). Prefix-final a coalesces with that augment to ā under 6.1.101. The lexical grade and the identity of aṅ distinguish this development from bhū’s sic-luk in the same line. Assign the ordinary active secondary endings.
