# PG23 — Effective markers and three different augment hosts

Author: Monty. 2 November 2026.

## M1. Historical p is not the effective pit query

Retain the original tiṅ, its markers and every later prescription.
Ordinary loṭ hi is apit by 3.4.87; first-person āṭ supplies pit by
3.4.92 in both padas. PG22's strong/weak stem partition follows.

For P vidhiliṅ, 3.4.103 explicitly supplies ṅit. **Tattvabodhinī
3.4.103** explains that this specific ṅit displaces the operative
pitness. **Kaumudī 2.4.53**, in its brū lexical discussion, also
uses the apit condition to exclude īṭ; Bālamanoramā identifies that
immediate explanation as the tātaṅ case. Together with the explicit
3.4.103 account this fixes the effective-pit query in both domains.
The host sūtra's vac substitution is a different, ārdhadhātuka
operation, not applied here.

Adopt that source account in this packet: keep historical_p=true
where appropriate, but give the source query effective_pit=false
after the explicit P-optative ṅit. A O endings are already apit and
ṅitvat. For B tātaṅ, PG20's separately sourced ṅit override likewise
displaces inherited tu-pitness. No earlier owner/history is erased.

This refines the earlier modal packets' treatment of a retained pit
flag plus a stronger ṅit guard. Those packets' weak-stem results remain;
the present construction makes the effective-pit query explicit because
**7.3.93 is an augment rule, not merely a guṇa rule**. A routine that
blocks guṇa while leaving raw pit operative for īṭ is insufficient.
The prescription is source/domain-specific, not a universal theorem about
every pair of markers in Sanskrit.

## M2. Rudādi iṭ is val-sensitive, not a strong-ending operation

The exact five D2 roots receive **7.2.76** iṭ before a val-initial
sārvadhātuka ending. Its ṭ makes it an initial part of that ending;
root and augment owners remain distinct. The guard admits weak endings
as well as strong ones. The iṭ does not itself convert the following
ending to pit or license guṇa that 1.1.5 prohibits.

Val excludes vowels **and y**. Thus ordinary loṭ consonant endings
receive iṭ, including hi and tām, but first-person āṭ and the vowel
third plural do not. O's resolved yā/yus boundary fails val, so all
five O profiles lack this augment. The guard is evaluated on the actual
following constituent, including the modal augment, not on an older bare t.

Examples with retained owners:

| Input | iṭ/strength decision | Output |
|---|---|---|
| rud∣tu | val true; strong | rod∣i∣tu → roditu |
| rud∣tām | val true; weak | rud∣i∣tām → ruditām |
| rud∣hi | val true; weak | rud∣i∣hi → rudihi |
| rud∣ā∣ni | vowel first; strong | rod∣ā∣ni → rodāni |
| rud∣yā∣t | y is not val; weak | rudyāt |
| rud∣tāt | val true; new weak tāt | ruditāt |

Kaumudī/Bālamanoramā 7.2.76 explicitly place iṭ before the attempted
6.4.101 dhi operation at hi. The intervening i defeats its consonant-
initial condition; **rudihi is not ruddhi**. The same local augment
reason applies to svap/śvas/an/jakṣ. No apṛkta īṭ under 7.3.98,
or imperfect 7.3.99/100 option, is imported into these moods.

## M3. Brū's īṭ requires effective pit and hal

**7.3.93** supplies ending-owned īṭ after exact brū before a
hal-initial pit sārvadhātuka. Its guard is

\[
\mathrm{Br\bar u}\land\mathrm{Sarva}\land
\mathrm{effective\_pit}\land\mathrm{hal\_first}.
\]

Hal includes y, unlike val. Consequently “y is not val” is not the
reason brūyāt lacks īṭ: M1's effective-pit override is decisive.
Ordinary brūhi is apit; brūtām and the A non-first endings are weak;
first-person āṭ begins with a vowel even though it is pit. Among the
new I/B ordinary slots only P 3sg has īṭ: brū→bro→brav before
owned ī, giving **bravītu**. Before first-person ā the same guṇa/av
path gives bravāṇi/bravai without any īṭ.

Tātaṅ is weak under its own source prescription, so **brūtāt** has
no īṭ. The historical p of original tu is not a fresh positive premise.
Neither brū's root ū nor īṭ's vowel can be confused with PG21's
u/śnu affix or PG23's different short iṭ.

## M4. Śī's guṇa overrides the ordinary weak prohibition

**7.4.21** gives śī→śe before sārvadhātuka, including kit/ṅit
contexts. Kaumudī/Bālamanoramā explicitly identify it as the exception
to 1.1.5. It does not change a weak ending into a strong ending.

Thus weak ordinary śī∣tām gives śetām; before vowel-initial modal
material e→ay by 6.1.78, giving **śayīta** and śayātām. A single
global “weak means no guṇa anywhere” toggle would lose this lexical
exception. Preserve the addressed source rule and its domain.

## M5. Ruṭ belongs to an actual jh-substitute

In śī's ordinary loṭ A 3pl, 7.1.5 gives **at** in the jh place.
**7.1.6** then inserts ruṭ at the beginning of that actual component,
giving the pronounced sequence śe∣r∣atām → **śeratām**. The r owns
its augment/source link. The teaching u is pronunciation-only and ṭ
is the it marker, as Bālamanoramā states.

In O, jha instead has **3.4.105 ran**. There is no at host for
7.1.6. The r in **śayīran** belongs to that ending, not to a ruṭ
carried over from śerate. Root guṇa and ruṭ insertion are separate
queries. This also prevents using śī's r insertion on brū's bruvatām.

## M6. Finite marker/augment completeness

**Proposition M6.** On D2, iṭ, īṭ and ruṭ have the exact
admission sets described in M2/M3/M5. Effective markers and the
following-sound view suffice together with their named lexical/host data.

**Proof.** D2 partitions the five rudādi roots, brū, śī and the
remaining han/śās. Inspect val versus hal/vowel at each prescribed
ending/augment boundary. M1 fixes pit/ṅit independently of pronunciation;
M4 supplies the one new lexical guṇa override. The ruṭ host exists
only at śī's ordinary loṭ A 3pl. These guards exhaust the admitted
augment rules; each event retains its distinct owner and source. □
