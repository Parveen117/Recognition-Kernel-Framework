# PG847 · Second and third in the oblique cases, and the seventy-first in two traditions

PG792 supplies 22 selected requests. PG846 already covers O20, including the long reading. The [remaining 21 profiles](PROFILES.tsv) use the [PG846 case endings](../ordinary_ordinal_cases_and_two_kathaka_tha_readings/ENDINGS.tsv) with the following two explicitly owned routes. In PG846 the heading and its state count twelve profiles. There are thirteen literal left pieces because O20 has two. All prior counts stand.

**O, the ḍaṭ family.** Concatenate any left piece in the row with each gender/number/case ending under its own column. Read | as alternative left pieces for a single request. The 5.2.53 words require a vatup-quantity input and ithuk. The many/how-many referents need an ordered series. Bare bahu with tithuk belongs to PG846. At 5.2.56 the O21–O31 profiles have the short form after loss of the final ṭi, or the tamaṭ history retaining the numeral before tama. The doubled t in triṃśattama is present on the long branch. At 5.2.57 the named higher numerals and the three time nouns require tamaṭ. A preceding numeral still permits the stipulated higher-numeral examples ekaśatatama and dviśatatama. Keep the count of the underlying expression as a supplied input. At 5.2.58 the simple sixty and seventy require tamaṭ. An earlier entire numeral in the compound removes that obligation and leaves the independent 5.2.56 option in force. Padamañjarī expressly distinguishes narrowing the immediately applicable rule from cancelling an earlier permission. These are ordinal tamaṭ plus ḍaṭ. Grade or superlative tamap has different accent and feminine licensing. A gendered bearer is supplied for the agreement construction; the cited example māsatamo divasaḥ itself has a masculine day. Neuter or feminine requests require a compatible bearer. Compare the positive feminine night below.

**T, the actual tīya suffix.** At 5.2.54–55 tīya is an exception replacing the *prescription* of ḍaṭ. Treating it as a substitute that inherits the ṭ marker would incorrectly give a long-ī feminine. Tattvabodhinī at 54 uses the attested rule wording dvitīyāśrita as a counterexample to that analysis. With tri, the r is vocalized and following vowel material contracts to give tṛ before tīya. No further lengthening of ṛ occurs. Feminine agreement is in ā. The pre-existing t of tīya blocks retroflexion across the earlier ṛ, so the regular instrumental is tṛtīyena.

For example, dvitīyasyai and dvitīyāyai share one feminine dative. Tṛtīyasmin and tṛtīye share one masculine or one neuter locative on an independently supplied bearer. The option is indexed by an original ṅ-marked ending. It supplies neither a masculine NOMpl in -e nor a pronominal GENpl in -eṣām. Source retention matters here. Kāśikā 7.3.115 gives the feminine optional syāṭ with shortening directly, and 1.1.36 quotes the supplementary tīya designation at ṅit with the two pairs of masculine dative forms. Tattvabodhinī explains how a shared vowel can still count with the earlier member, and how the supplement needed for masculine and neuter can also explain the feminine. Haradatta's Padamañjarī 7.3.115 objects to discarding a sūtra by appeal to a supplement. It then proposes an alternative analysis using the short stems and transfer of the relevant pronominal operations, which could dispense with the supplement. Keep that proposal as a commentary policy. Three derivational accounts of one dative never make three new words.

**Two settings for the feminine locative.** In KS 2.9 the text addresses Agni Aṅgiras and specifies the second and third earths with dvitīyasyām and tṛtīyasyām. KS 25.6 quotes the formula in explaining the relevant worlds. At 23.10 the same third locative agrees with div, ‘sky’, where Soma is to be fetched. At 30.10 Gāyatrī brings him from the third heaven. The last two passages do not have the word for earth. These are witness-specific positive uses of the same ending. The regular masculine locative tṛtīye has an independent example in Bhāgavata 1.4.14 with yugaparyaye. Its mundane chronology supplies no licence to alter the text of the Kāṭhaka.

**The end of whose year?** Mādhyandina Śatapatha 11.5.1.11 has Urvaśī tell Purūravas to come on the year-completing night. The accusative feminine saṃvatsaratamīm agrees with rātrim. The commentary in this witness explicitly explains the ordinal as the last night completing the year. The feminine -ī therefore has a directly attested temporal bearer. Optional short forms of an ordinary decade do not license omission of tamaṭ in this named time construction.

**Which word completes seventy-one?** Two digitizations of Vasu's 1897 Book V show the same printed p. 918. In the short prefixed seventy example the final consonant before nominative -aḥ is **m**, verifying the diagnostic part of the archived Vasu *ekasaptamaḥ* reading. These are two copies of the same edition. They supply no independent derivation of that m. The Kāśikā reads *ekasaptataḥ*. On the short ḍaṭ path final ti loses its last vowel, yielding ekasaptat-a. The n-final guard for maṭ fails. The Ujjain Vedic education board's Sanskrit text, printed p. 42, PDF page 68, gives the short forms ekasaptataḥ, ekasaptatī and ekasaptatam next to the long forms. Select the t-stem for grammatical extension. The m-reading stays Accepted at its witnessed masculine nominative. Its origin and independent licensing in other cases stay Open. Agreement on the long form does not resolve how the short printed m arose.
