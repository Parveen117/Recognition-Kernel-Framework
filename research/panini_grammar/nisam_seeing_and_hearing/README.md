# PG209 · Ni with śam in seeing and hearing

Monty · 12 November 2026

Puruṣakāra on *Daiva* 149 gives two complete third-person singular examples with the **same prefix**. Someone sees a form with **niśāmayati rūpam** and hears a verse with **niśamayati ślokam**. It also gives unprefixed **śamayati rogam** for alleviating a disease. The semantic distinction affects the vowel inside the verb.

| In the commentary | Meaning | mit on the selected account | Present 3sg | Past *laṅ* 3sg |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *ni* + śam, *rūpam* | see a form | excluded | niśāmayati | nyaśāmayat |
| *ni* + śam, *ślokam* | hear a verse | admitted | niśamayati | nyaśamayat |

The gaṇasūtra *śamo darśane* (01.0938 in the pinned Dhātupāṭha) inherits the negation from the preceding context. Under the reading selected here it excludes *mit* when the meaning is seeing. The causative then retains the long vowel under 7.2.116. In the hearing example, 6.4.92 shortens the penultimate vowel before ṇic. The rule labels do not change the *ś* of the root. In *laṅ* the augment stands after *ni*. The combination of *ni* and *a* gives *nya* by 6.1.77. All 72 unprefixed components of the two four-lakāra schedules have a counterpart under source index 01.0929. The prefixed words and their contextual assignments are the written construction here.

The two grammatical examples alone do not settle literary usage. In Bhāgavata 4.29.52 Nārada refers to himself as speaking, and **niśāmaya**, with long *ā*, asks the listener to hear a secret. That is a separate, attested reading of the second-singular imperative. A reader who forced the commentator’s short-vowel hearing example on every text would misread this verse. The literary attestation is attached to its own context without adding a second set of inflectional positions.
