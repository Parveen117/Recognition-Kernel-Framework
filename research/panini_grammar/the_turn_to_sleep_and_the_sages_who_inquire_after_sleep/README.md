# PG862 · A turn to sleep and the sages who ask about sleep in Raghuvaṃśa 10.14

### Who has the turn?

At 3.3.38 Kāśikā distinguishes taking one's place in a sequence from letting time pass. At 3.3.39 the preverb is vi or upa, the root is śīṅ, and taking a turn is the condition for ghañ. The ṅ of the lexical root remains source information. It is the ñ of the affix that conditions vṛddhi under 7.2.115. Final ī reaches ai, then āy before the residual vowel under 6.1.78. In the ordinary non-turn controls ac under 3.3.56 instead gives guṇa e and then ay. This yields the contrast viśāya/viśaya and upaśāya/upaśaya. Padamañjarī actually glosses the short-vowel viśaya as **doubt** and upaśaya as **lying nearby**. The former must not automatically be glossed as an ordinary rest. Its dental palatal ś is distinct from ṣ in viṣaya.

Three sentence frames distinguish the relevant participants. (1) In Kāśikā's *tava rājopaśāyaḥ*, *tava* identifies whose turn it is. In its expansion *rājānam upaśayitum* the accusative identifies the king attended. Haradatta relates the infinitive to an opportunity, comparing the opportunity or occasion to eat. (2) In Mādhavīya p. 340, *tavopaśāyaḥ* resolves as *tava upaśāyaḥ*. No king is specified in this example. (3) In the same print *upaśāyo vartate* presents the turn-taking as pervasive. The witness explicitly says that ghañ prevails even in the presence of the later inuṇ of 3.3.44. Preserve the external sandhi separately from the word upaśāyaḥ.

The later rule ordinarily selects inuṇ for a pervasive action and the Kāśikā there discusses the separate admission of lyuṭ. On the overlapping turn-and-pervasiveness input Haradatta explicitly identifies the defeated rival as inuṇ. He explains why repeating the turn condition in 3.3.39 does work even though the preceding rule supplied succession. Mādhavīya independently names the same rival. The pinned Nyāsa instead has the string *parimāṇam api* at this point. It supports the argument from otherwise redundant wording. Its exact lexical reading and the relationship to the other witnesses remain open. No corrected word has been substituted in its source record. Neither commentary makes every pervasive action a turn.

### Who asks, and who slept?

In Raghuvaṃśa 10.7 the gods see Viṣṇu. The accusative *tam* supplies the object still described in 10.14. There *anugṛhṇantam* is masculine accusative singular and describes Viṣṇu showing favour. The instrumental plural *avalokanaiḥ* identifies the glances by which he does so. *Bhṛgvādīn … saukhaśāyanikān ṛṣīn* is the masculine accusative plural group receiving the favour. The sages headed by Bhṛgu are the people asking about Viṣṇu's sleep. They are the agents in Mallinātha's explanatory *pṛcchanti*, while in the poem their case belongs to the object of the participle. Kale's edition prints both verse and commentary on Sanskrit p. 225 (PDF page 279). The published translation in the same volume independently makes this participant structure explicit as a translation, on PDF page 379. The GRETIL line has an editorial space within the word. The printed verse and the commentary give the compound intact.

Kāśikā under 4.4.1 and the vārttika cited in Bālamanoramā under 7.3.7 admit ṭhak for the one who asks about *sukhaśayanam*. The accusative is the object inside the explanatory clause. The compound supplies the base of the new agent designation. Under 7.3.20 the anuśatikādi membership licenses the initial vṛddhi of both members, su → sau and śa → śā. Rule 7.3.50 gives the ika substitution. The case of the resulting agent noun is independently assigned in the poem. Śrīvallabha on Haimanāmamālāśiloñcha 69 identifies the same two growth operations but cites **Haima 6.4.42, ikaṇ**. The verse lists two other words for the herald, based respectively on the bed and on having slept well. These are lexical alternatives. There is no replacement of the Pāṇinian affix by the Haima affix and the poetic sages need not have held a court office.
